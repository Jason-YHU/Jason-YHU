12.2 Centripetal Acceleration & Force

high-yield
Concept A force of constant magnitude, always perpendicular to the direction of motion, causes centripetal acceleration — and this keeps an object moving in a circle at constant angular speed. This force always points towards the centre; the object's velocity is always tangent to the circle, so acceleration and velocity are always at 90° to each other.
Centripetal acceleration
a = v²/r   =   ω²r

(substituting v = ωr)

Wherea = centripetal acceleration, directed towards the centre (m s⁻²) · v = linear speed (m s⁻¹) · ω = angular velocity (rad s⁻¹) · r = radius of the circular path (m)
Centripetal force (F = ma)
F = mv²/r   =   mω²r
WhereF = centripetal force — the resultant force towards the centre (N) · m = mass of the object (kg) · v = linear speed (m s⁻¹) · ω = angular velocity (rad s⁻¹) · r = radius (m)

Use v²/r when you know the linear speed, and ω²r when you know the angular velocity (e.g. from a period).

Formula

Whereg = gravitational field strength (N kg⁻¹) · F = gravitational force on the mass (N) · m = the small mass placed in the field (kg)

13.2 Gravitational Force Between Point Masses

high-yield
Newton's law of gravitation Every object attracts every other object with an attractive force which is directly proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the separation between them.
Formula
F = G m₁ m₂ / r²
WhereF = gravitational force between the two masses (N) · G = universal gravitational constant, 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² · m₁, m₂ = the two masses (kg) · r = separation of the two objects, centre to centre (m)The minus sign in your notes, F = −Gm₁m₂/r², only says the force is attractive — directed towards the other mass. The syllabus uses the magnitude.
Corrected You wrote G = 6.67×1011 N m² kg2. It should be 6.67×10−11 N m² kg−2 — the exponent is negative (G is tiny, which is why gravity only becomes noticeable when at least one mass is planet-sized) and the unit has kg−2. You can check the unit by rearranging G = Fr²/(m₁m₂): N m² / kg².
Spheres act like point masses For a point outside a uniform sphere, the mass of the sphere can be treated as a point mass at its centre. That's why r is always measured to the centre — e.g. for a satellite, r = radius of Earth + height above the surface.

Circular orbits

Your notes stop before orbits. In a circular orbit, gravity is the force that provides the centripetal force (Topic 12):
F (gravity) acts towards the centre; v is along the tangent — always at 90°.
  1. Gravity = centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r
  2. Cancel m and one r: v² = GM/r, so v = √(GM/r) — the orbital speed doesn't depend on the satellite's mass.
  3. Use v = 2πr/T: (2πr/T)² = GM/r, so T² = 4π²r³ / (GM) — i.e. T² ∝ r³.
WhereG = gravitational constant (N m² kg⁻²) · M = mass of the body being orbited (kg) · m = mass of the satellite (kg) · r = orbital radius, centre to centre (m) · v = orbital speed (m s⁻¹) · T = orbital period (s)

14.1 Thermal Equilibrium

Definition Thermal energy transfers from regions of higher temperature to regions of lower temperature, due to particle collisions that transfer energy. Regions of equal temperature are in thermal equilibrium — there is no net flow of thermal energy between them.
Self-testAt thermal equilibrium, does energy stop being transferred entirely?
No — collisions (and hence energy transfer) still happen both ways, but there's no net flow because both directions balance out.

14.3 Specific Heat Capacity & Specific Latent Heat

high-yield
Definition — specific heat capacity The amount of energy required to increase the temperature of 1 kg of a material by 1 °C (equivalently, 1 K).
Formula
c = ΔQ / (mΔT)
Wherec = specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) · ΔQ = energy transferred as heat (J) · m = mass (kg) · ΔT = temperature change = Tfinal − Tinitial (K or °C)

Mixing materials

Principle Thermal energy transfers from the hotter object to the colder object until both reach the same final temperature.
Conservation of energy
−ΔEhot = ΔEcold
WhereΔEhot = energy change of the hotter object (J) — negative, since it loses energy · ΔEcold = energy change of the colder object (J) — positive, since it gains energyFor each object, ΔE = mcΔT.

Wheremc = mass of the copper ball (kg) · 385, 4200 = specific heat capacities of copper and water (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) · 0.7 = mass of the water (kg) · (80 − 700), (80 − 20) = ΔT = final − initial temperature (°C)
Definition — specific latent heat The energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance, with no change in temperature.
Latent heat of fusion / vaporisation Fusion: energy to turn 1 kg of a substance from solid ⇔ liquid.
Vaporisation: energy to turn 1 kg of a substance from liquid ⇔ gas.

Derivation — you flagged this one to add ("Claude — add to notes") Consider N molecules, each of mass m, bouncing around a cube of side L (volume V = L³). Look at one molecule with velocity component cx perpendicular to one pair of walls.
  1. One collision: the wall is rigid and the collision elastic, so the molecule rebounds with its speed unchanged, direction reversed. Change in momentum = m cx − (−m cx) = 2m cx.
  2. Time between hits on that same wall: the molecule must cross the cube and back, a distance 2L, at speed cx → time = 2L / cx.
  3. Force from one molecule = rate of change of momentum = 2m cx ÷ (2L / cx) = m cx² / L.
  4. Force from all N molecules on that wall = N m ⟨cx²⟩ / L (using the mean-square, since molecules don't all move at the same speed).
  5. Pressure = force ÷ area = [N m ⟨cx²⟩ / L] ÷ L² = N m ⟨cx²⟩ / V.

WhereEP = electric potential energy of the pair (J) · Q, q = the two charges (C), with their signs · r = their separation (m) · V = potential at q's position due to Q (V)

Quiz — Topic 18

1. Define electric field strength.
The force per unit positive charge exerted on a positive charge placed at that point (E = F/q, units N C⁻¹).
2. A 2.0 μC charge sits in a field of 5.0×10³ N C⁻¹. What force acts on it?
F = qE = 2.0×10⁻⁶ × 5.0×10³ = 1.0×10⁻² N.
3. Two parallel plates 4.0 cm apart have a p.d. of 200 V across them. Find the field strength between them.

4. An electron enters the uniform field between two plates. Which way does it accelerate, relative to the field lines?
Opposite to the field direction (towards the positive plate), because its charge is negative: F = qE with q < 0.
5. Why can field lines never cross?
The field has only one direction at any point — crossing lines would mean two different force directions at the same point.
6. State Coulomb's law.
The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation: F = Q₁Q₂/(4πε₀r²).

It falls to 1/9 (F ∝ 1/r²).

E = kQ/r² = (9.0×10⁹ × 6.0×10⁻⁹) / (0.30)² = 6.0×10² N C⁻¹, directed radially away from the charge.
10. Why can a charged metal sphere be treated as a point charge at its centre?
Outside the sphere the charge is spread symmetrically, so the field there is identical to that of all the charge placed at the centre. r is measured from the centre.

The work done per unit positive charge in bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to that point (V = W/q).

It only applies to a uniform field (parallel plates). A point charge's field is radial and gets weaker with distance (E ∝ 1/r²), so use E = Q/(4πε₀r²) or the gradient of the V–r graph.
