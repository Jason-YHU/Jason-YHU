12.2 Centripetal Acceleration & Force

high-yield
Concept A force of constant magnitude, always perpendicular to the direction of motion, causes centripetal acceleration — and this keeps an object moving in a circle at constant angular speed. This force always points towards the centre; the object's velocity is always tangent to the circle, so acceleration and velocity are always at 90° to each other.
Centripetal acceleration
a = v²/r   =   ω²r

(substituting v = ωr)

Centripetal force (F = ma)
F = mv²/r   =   mω²r

Use v²/r when you know the linear speed, and ω²r when you know the angular velocity (e.g. from a period).

Quiz — Topic 12

1. Convert 3π/4 rad to degrees.
135°
2. A satellite orbits with ω = 1.2×10⁻³ rad s⁻¹ at radius 7000 km. Find its speed.
v = ωr = 1.2×10⁻³ × 7.0×10⁶ = 8400 m s⁻¹
3. Why is a satellite in circular orbit accelerating even though its speed is constant?
Its velocity's direction is constantly changing, and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity (a vector) — a constant-magnitude, always-perpendicular force (gravity) causes this.
4. A ball on a string is swung in a vertical circle. At the top of the circle, what two forces provide the centripetal force?
Tension in the string and the ball's weight — both act towards the centre at the top of the circle, so T + mg = mv²/r there.
5. Double the radius of a circular path while keeping speed v constant. What happens to the centripetal acceleration?
It halves (a = v²/r, so a is inversely proportional to r).

14.1 Thermal Equilibrium

Definition Thermal energy transfers from regions of higher temperature to regions of lower temperature, due to particle collisions that transfer energy. Regions of equal temperature are in thermal equilibrium — there is no net flow of thermal energy between them.
Self-testAt thermal equilibrium, does energy stop being transferred entirely?
No — collisions (and hence energy transfer) still happen both ways, but there's no net flow because both directions balance out.

14.3 Specific Heat Capacity & Specific Latent Heat

high-yield
Definition — specific heat capacity The amount of energy required to increase the temperature of 1 kg of a material by 1 °C (equivalently, 1 K).
Formula
c = ΔQ / (mΔT)

units: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹  ·  ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial

Mixing materials

Principle Thermal energy transfers from the hotter object to the colder object until both reach the same final temperature.
Conservation of energy
−ΔEhot = ΔEcold
Worked example — copper ball (700 °C) dropped into 700 g water (20 °C), equilibrium at 80 °C cwater = 4200 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹, ccopper = 385 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹. Find the mass of the copper ball.
−(mc × 385 × (80 − 700)) = 0.7 × 4200 × (80 − 20)
mc × 385 × 620 = 176 400  →  mc ≈ 0.74 kg
Definition — specific latent heat The energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance, with no change in temperature.
Latent heat of fusion / vaporisation Fusion: energy to turn 1 kg of a substance from solid ⇔ liquid.
Vaporisation: energy to turn 1 kg of a substance from liquid ⇔ gas.
Self-testΔT is T₂ − T₁ or T₁ − T₂?

15.3 Kinetic Theory of Gases

Assumptions of an ideal gas

  • Particles have negligible volume compared to the volume of the gas
  • Collisions (between particles, and with the container) are perfectly elastic
  • Intermolecular forces can be ignored, except during collisions
  • There is a very large number of particles
  • Particles move in random directions with random speeds
  • The time of a collision is negligible compared to the time between collisions
Corrected Your last assumption said "they obey gas laws" — but the gas laws are a consequence of kinetic theory, not an assumption you'd use to derive it (that would be circular reasoning). The actual assumption is that particles obey Newton's laws of motion.
high-yield
Derivation — you flagged this one to add ("Claude — add to notes") Consider N molecules, each of mass m, bouncing around a cube of side L (volume V = L³). Look at one molecule with velocity component cx perpendicular to one pair of walls.
  1. One collision: the wall is rigid and the collision elastic, so the molecule rebounds with its speed unchanged, direction reversed. Change in momentum = m cx − (−m cx) = 2m cx.
  2. Time between hits on that same wall: the molecule must cross the cube and back, a distance 2L, at speed cx → time = 2L / cx.
  3. Force from one molecule = rate of change of momentum = 2m cx ÷ (2L / cx) = m cx² / L.
  4. Force from all N molecules on that wall = N m ⟨cx²⟩ / L (using the mean-square, since molecules don't all move at the same speed).
  5. Pressure = force ÷ area = [N m ⟨cx²⟩ / L] ÷ L² = N m ⟨cx²⟩ / V.
  6. Extend from 1D to 3D: motion is random, so on average ⟨cx²⟩ = ⟨cy²⟩ = ⟨cz²⟩. Since c² = cx² + cy² + cz², this means ⟨c²⟩ = 3⟨cx²⟩, so ⟨cx²⟩ = ⅓⟨c²⟩.
Substituting into step 5:
p = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ / V   ⇔   pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩
Pressure of an ideal gas
pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩

N = number of molecules · m = mass of one molecule · ⟨c²⟩ = mean-square speed

Worked example — finding c.r.m.s. N = 3.0×10²³ molecules, m = 5.0×10⁻²⁶ kg each, V = 2.0×10⁻³ m³, p = 1.5×10⁵ Pa.
⟨c²⟩ = 3pV / (Nm) = (3 × 1.5×10⁵ × 2.0×10⁻³) / (3.0×10²³ × 5.0×10⁻²⁶) = 900 / 0.015 = 6.0×10⁴ m² s⁻²
cr.m.s. = √(6.0×10⁴) ≈ 245 m s⁻¹
Your notes titled this section "root-mean-square speed" but stopped after the pressure equation — here's the rest:
Root-mean-square speed
cr.m.s. = √⟨c²⟩

— the square root of the mean of the squared speeds (not the mean speed itself, and not the square of the mean speed).

Average kinetic energy of a molecule Comparing pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ with pV = NkT gives:
½m⟨c²⟩ = 3⁄2kT   =   3⁄2 · RT⁄NA

(the second form just substitutes k = R/NA — useful if you're given R and NA rather than k directly). This is a key result: average molecular KE depends only on thermodynamic temperature, nothing else.

Self-testIs cr.m.s. the same as the mean speed of the particles?
No — it's the square root of the mean of the squared speeds, which is always slightly higher than the plain mean speed.

16.1 Internal Energy

high-yield
Definition — temperature The measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
Definition — internal energy The sum of the kinetic energies and potential energies of the particles moving randomly in a substance. A rise in an object's temperature means a rise in its internal energy.
Putting it together
Internal energy = (KE of molecules) + (PE of bonds)

The heating curve

RegionWhat's happening
A — solid warmingTemperature rises. Particles vibrate more vigorously about fixed positions → average KE increases.
B — melting (plateau)Change of state: energy breaks/weakens bonds, not raising temperature. KE constant; PE increases.
C — liquid warmingTemperature rises again — same mechanism as A, now in the liquid phase.
D — boiling (plateau)Bonds are broken completely as liquid becomes gas. KE constant; PE increases (its biggest jump).
E — gas warmingTemperature continues to rise as the gas is heated further; average KE keeps increasing.

16.2 The First Law of Thermodynamics

high-yield
Deriving work done, W = pΔV Start from basic mechanics: W = Fs (work = force × distance moved by a piston).
Since p = F/A, we get F = pA. Substituting:
W = pAs

A × s = the volume swept out by the piston, ΔV, so:

W = pΔV
Sign convention Gas expansion: the gas pushes outward and does work on its surroundings → W (work done on the gas) is negative.
Gas compression: the surroundings push in and do work on the gas → W is positive.
First law of thermodynamics
ΔU = q + W

ΔU = the increase in internal energy of the gas · q = energy transferred to the system via heating · W = work done on the system (see sign convention above).

Worked example A gas absorbs 500 J of heat and expands, doing 200 J of work on its surroundings. Find ΔU.
Expansion → work done on the gas is negative: W = −200 J  →  ΔU = 500 + (−200) = 300 J
Self-testA gas is compressed and cooled. What are the signs of q and W in ΔU = q + W?
q is negative (heat leaves the system), W is positive (work is done on the gas by compressing it).

Quiz — Topic 16

It increased — a temperature rise means the average KE of its particles increased, which increases internal energy.

Because the equation being plotted is ln I = ln I₀ + (gradient)×t — the intercept is whatever "c" is in y = mx + c, and here y = ln I, so c = ln I₀.
